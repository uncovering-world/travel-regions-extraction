# How long forcible territorial change takes to settle — literature

**Research note, not normative.** Issue: [#20](https://github.com/uncovering-world/travel-regions-extraction/issues/20). Compiled on 2026-10-03 by a research subagent for the question of how long contested control must last before the canon follows it; sources opened that day.

How to read it:

- A page or table reference means the source states it.
- **[C]** marks a computation made during the review from the source's own table or data, not a published figure. Durations from datasets with year precision are differences of calendar years, so "within 2 years" can mean almost 3.
- **[I]** marks an inference of the review.
- **Checked again when the note was filed:** the quotations from Altman 2020, Kolstø 2006, Florea 2020, Koutroulis 2012, Collier et al. 2006 and Fortna 2003 were matched against the source texts; the distribution of loss years in the Modern Conquest data and the transcription of Florea's Table 2 are reproduced by the [settling-rule experiment](../../experiments/settling-rule/README.md). **Not checked again, so unverified:** the other [C] figures (Edelstein's appendix, the 1918–1945 contrast, Territorial Contenders, Fortna's appendix, the Frozen Conflict Dataset, war durations).
- Caspersen's two-year criterion is second-hand; the reason for Florea's 24 months was not found.

## 1. Military occupations
Edelstein 2004 [1]: 24 occupations since 1815 — 7 successes, 4 mixed, 13 failures (p. 57). Annexations and "short-term interventions" are excluded by definition (p. 52), so quick reversals cannot appear. [C] Appendix 1 (pp. 84–90): the 23 concluded occupations lasted 3–72 years, median 10; none ended within 2 years, 7 within 5. No other occupation dataset could be opened.

## 2. Conquest after 1945
- Altman 2020 [2]: since 1945, 4 attempts on entire states and 65 on parts (p. 501); about half of the latter held (Table 5, p. 509). P. 502: "conquest attempts tend to either fail quickly or succeed … Of the thirty-four conquest attempts since 1945 in which the challenger 'succeeded' by possessing the territory when the immediate conflict subsided, challengers had lost control ten years later in only five cases."
- Altman 2017 [3], Table 3 (p. 888): of 112 land grabs 1918–2016, 59 were held when the dispute ended, 47 for ten uninterrupted years (post-2006 cases omitted).
- Zacher 2001 [4]: 40 territorial aggressions 1946–2000, 17 changing territory (p. 228); nothing on reversal timing.
- [C] Modern Conquest v3.0 [5] (year precision), 78 initial attempts 1946–2024 with coded outcome (Ukraine 2022 uncoded): 34 were lost before the immediate conflict ended — 26 in the seizure year, 7 the next, 1 three years on. Of 44 held at conflict end, 10 were lost later, after 2, 2, 3, 6, 9, 9, 15, 15, 24 and 29 years (five by international ruling, two by force). So 35 of 44 losses came within two years, 37 within five. Attempts to 2004 still held after 2 years: 15% lost by year 10 (n=34); after 5 years: 9% (n=32). Populated territories (n=47) behave alike.
- [C] 1918–1945 contrast [5]: of 31 attempts still held after 2 years, 26 were later lost, 20 of them in years 3–6.

## 3. De facto states
- Florea 2017 [6]: 34 cases 1945–2011; "median survival time … is 345 months", range 25–756 (manuscript p. 17). Florea 2020 [7] (p. 1015): 40 through 2016 — 6 forcefully and 6 peacefully reintegrated, 4 became states, 24 alive. Hazard by age is not reported.
- [C] [7] Table 2: the 16 that ended lasted 3–55 years (median 10.5), six only 3–4 years — about 6% per year at ages 2–5, 1.5% later. Every case is at least 2 years old by definition.
- Territorial contenders (any non-state control of populated territory), Kenwick & Lemke 2023 [8]: of 145 post-WWII cases, 50 were forcefully and 43 peacefully reintegrated (p. 486); they "only live for an average of about ten years" (p. 492). [C] Data [9], 147 born 1946+: 18% gone within 2 calendar years, 39% within 5, 56% within 10; annual hazard about 9–10% in years 1–5, 6% in years 6–20, 3.5% later.

## 4. Ceasefires and frozen lines
- Fortna 2003 [10]: 48 interstate cease-fire dyads 1946–97; "War resumes eventually in twenty-one cases" (p. 350); hazard "decreasing" but "not always statistically significant" (p. 365). [C] Appendix 1: 7 of the 21 failures came within 3 months, 8 within 2 years, 9 within 5; of 40 cease-fires that held 2 years, 13 later saw war resume. Arab–Israeli dyads dominate.
- Clayton et al. 2023 [11]: 2,202 civil-conflict ceasefires; failed ones last a median 65 days until 25 fatalities (p. 1444).
- Collier et al. 2006 [12]: after civil wars, reversion risk 23% in the first four years, 17% in the next six — "no 'safe period' during the decade" (p. 9).
- Frozen Conflict Dataset [13] [C]: of 42 cases 1946–2011, 19 ended (4 violently) after a median 15 years; 23 ongoing, median age 33. Selected on persistence.

## 5. War duration
[C] COW Inter-State War Data v4.0 [14], 95 wars: median 162 days; 68% over within 1 year, 85% within 2, 95% within 5. The 38 begun after 1945: median 102 days; 74%, 87%, 92%; longest Vietnam 10.2 and Iran–Iraq 7.9 years.

## 6. Thresholds in use
- **2 years.** Kolstø 2006 [15] (p. 726): "to eliminate a whole spate of ephemeral political contraptions, I exclude those that have persisted in this state of non-recognition for less than two years" — the only justification found. Florea [6][7]: "exists for at least 24 months", no reason given. Caspersen 2012: "existed for at least two years", seen only as quoted in [16]. Pegg 1998 [17] has no number: control "maintained for an extended period of time".
- **5 years.** Roberts: prolonged occupation "lasts more than 5 years and extends into a period when hostilities are sharply reduced"; any such definition "will essentially be arbitrary" (Koutroulis 2012 [18], p. 168).
- **Others.** 10 years: Altman's HELD10 [5]. 1 year: UCDP conflict episodes (Kreutz 2010 [19], p. 244). None: a frozen conflict starts the year the war ends [13]. Fortna (p. 348) calls a fixed cut-off ("five years, say") "arbitrary".

## Synthesis
**Sources say:** interstate seizures fail fast or stick; once the taker holds the territory when the immediate conflict ends, loss within ten years is uncommon (5 of 34). Non-state control is far less durable. Year thresholds are conventions their own authors call pragmatic or arbitrary.

**Inference [I]:**
1. Count from the end of fighting over the territory, not from seizure. After 1945 the two nearly coincide, and two years capture 35 of the 37 reversals that five years would. In a long war two years of control mean little (1918–1945: 84% later lost); 13% of post-1945 wars outlast two years.
2. For interstate seizures, two years after hostilities end is defensible; five years lowers the ten-year reversal risk only from about 15% to 9%. Later reversals come after 6–29 years, mostly by ruling, negotiation or withdrawal rather than force, so they call for a re-evaluation path, not a longer wait.
3. For breakaway entities neither two nor five years marks stability: 46% of contenders surviving two years, and 27% of those surviving five, are gone by year ten. The two-year convention defines "exists as a de facto state", not "settled".
4. An intermediate "unsettled" status is supported: during hostilities, for about two years after them, and while control rests with an unrecognised breakaway.

**Strength:** moderate for interstate seizures (one author's dataset, n=78, year precision, computations not independently reviewed) and contenders (sample of 62 states, includes rebel zones); weak for de facto states (n≤40, truncated at two years), occupations (n=24, selected) and ceasefires (clustered dyads).
